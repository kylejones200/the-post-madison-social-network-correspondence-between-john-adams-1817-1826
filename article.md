### The Post-Madison Social Network: Correspondence Between John Adams, 1817--1826
After the final term of James Madison, the American experiment
continued --- in letters. From 1817 to his death in 1826, John Adams
remained one of the most active voices in the country's political and
philosophical life. He wrote. A lot.

Thanks to the [Founders Online
Corpus](https://founders.archives.gov/), we can trace that activity, message by
message. This dataset collects and standardizes over 185,000 documents
from key American founders. Each record includes metadata like sender,
recipient, date, and full text. Most come from the major
editions --- *The Adams Papers*, *The Papers of Thomas Jefferson*, and
others --- published by university presses and digitized by the U.S.
National Archives.

The post-Madison slice of this corpus shows what comes after formal
power. What happens when men who helped design a country step back and
just talk? And who do they talk to?

Let's start with John Adams.

Between 1817 and 1826, Adams sent 988 letters and received 1,006. He was
not writing in a vacuum. He corresponded with over 1,700 individuals,
including relatives, former rivals, allies, editors, and friends. His
longest-running conversations stretched for decades. His shortest were
short because someone else stopped writing back. Across all this, Adams
wrote over 329,000 words --- not counting the thousands he read from
others.

That's engagement. At scale.

To make sense of this volume, we can use tools from network analysis.
This approach treats people as nodes and letters as connections. It
turns qualitative historical content into a quantitative social
structure. The more messages between two people, the stronger the tie.
The more people a figure corresponds with, the higher their degree of
centrality.

In the post-Madison corpus, Thomas Jefferson is the most connected node
in the graph. But here's the twist: John Adams and Jefferson share the
same network. They reach the same 1,708 people. There are no connections
unique to Adams, and none unique to Jefferson. Different personalities,
different views --- but in this period, they're operating inside the
same social universe.

It's not that they wrote the same things. But they were both part of the
same ongoing conversation.

To capture what that felt like on the ground --- how letters moved, day
by day --- I built a short animation of Adams's outbound messages. Each
red streak shows a letter flying outward from Quincy, Massachusetts to
its recipient. Some days were quiet. Others were a frenzy.


The Founders Online Corpus preserves a record of this unusual social
network of the founders of the US. These leaders were nodes in a lively
network, writing across time and distance with urgency and care. Adams
wasn't writing history. He was writing relationships. And the record is
long enough to trace.
